A district cannot legitimately have more Aadhaar cards than people living in it. Yet several border districts in Bihar are showing saturation rates above 120% — a number that only makes sense if it is being gamed.

<SectionHeading>What the SIR Is Trying to Fix</SectionHeading>

Bihar's Special Intensive Revision (SIR) requires nearly 8 crore voters to resubmit documentation, with anyone registered after 2003 needing to provide citizenship proof — not just an Aadhaar or ration card, both of which the government and the Supreme Court have separately clarified are not proof of citizenship at all.

<Definition term="Aadhaar Card">India's unique identification number, functionally equivalent to a US Social Security Number — proof of identity, explicitly not proof of citizenship, and issuable from birth with no minimum age requirement.</Definition>

<Definition term="Special Intensive Revision (SIR)">An Election Commission exercise requiring voters to re-verify eligibility with documentation beyond Aadhaar or ration cards, aimed at closing the gap that has allowed non-citizens onto voter rolls.</Definition>

<Callout type="warning">
The CEO of the UIDAI — the body that issues Aadhaar — has stated plainly that Aadhaar was never intended as proof of citizenship. A Supreme Court suggestion that it be accepted as valid SIR proof does not override this, and remains non-binding on the Election Commission.
</Callout>

<SectionHeading>The Numbers That Don't Add Up</SectionHeading>

Bihar's statewide Aadhaar saturation sits around 94% — already unusually high. But broken down by district, a clear pattern emerges.

<Pullquote>The higher the Muslim population share in a border district, the higher the Aadhaar saturation — a correlation too consistent to be coincidence.</Pullquote>

West Bengal's numbers are more striking still: an estimated population of 100.04 million against 105 million Aadhaar cards issued — a 105.7% saturation rate, calculated before any door-to-door verification exercise has even begun. Districts bordering Bangladesh — Malda, Murshidabad, North and South Dinajpur, 24 Parganas — are flagged as the most likely concentration points.

<Divider />

<SectionHeading>A Pattern, Not a One-Off</SectionHeading>

This isn't a new observation. A similar anomaly was previously documented and publicly flagged nearly a year earlier during analysis of Jharkhand's elections, where voter turnout in certain Christian- and Muslim-majority pockets ran far above statistical norms — alongside evidence of the same population appearing registered in multiple constituencies.

<Callout type="note">
Two states already stand out for statistically improbable turnout figures near 80%: Andhra Pradesh and West Bengal. When combined with multiple Aadhaar registrations, that pattern points to strategic movement of voters between constituencies once a winning threshold is secured in one seat.
</Callout>

The mechanics described are specific: once a bloc has enough registered voters to tip a constituency, that number tends to plateau across subsequent elections — because the group has already achieved control — while the surplus registrations continue migrating to flip the next constituency. None of this works without duplicate registration, which Aadhaar's uneven rollout — inconsistent use of retinal biometrics, fingerprints that fade over time — made easier than it should have been.

<Divider />

<SectionHeading>Beyond the Voter Roll: Subsidies and Census</SectionHeading>

The consequences extend past elections. Duplicate or fraudulent Aadhaar registrations also unlock free ration allocations, cash transfer schemes, and MGNREGA payments — a drain estimated in the hundreds of crores, diverted from programs that could otherwise fund development.

<Callout type="tip">
An official 2016 parliamentary estimate placed known illegal immigrants at 20 million, concentrated disproportionately in Assam, West Bengal, Bihar, and Uttar Pradesh — with the real, undocumented number likely higher.
</Callout>

The same distortion shows up in census data: the recorded Christian population share was 2.34% in 1951 and 2.3% in 2011 — essentially flat across seventy years of documented conversion activity, suggesting the census figures themselves carry the same kind of undercount or manipulation as the fertility-rate data examined in earlier analysis.

<Divider />

<SectionHeading>Local Complicity and the Path Forward</SectionHeading>

Part of the problem sits at the booth level itself — officers responsible for running elections have, in specific documented cases, been found compromised, sometimes allegedly cutting power to sections that voted against the locally dominant party after results were tallied.

<Pullquote>The Election Commission taking this move — after nearly a year of data-driven public pressure — is a genuinely good sign, even if the corruption runs deep at almost every level below it.</Pullquote>

The Election Commission's current three-person appointment structure — a Union minister, the Prime Minister, and the Leader of Opposition jointly recommending appointments since 2023 — is a structural improvement over the earlier system, where the Prime Minister alone made the call. Whether that structural fix translates into cleaner rolls before West Bengal, Assam, Kerala, and Tamil Nadu head to their own elections is the open question — and the border districts making the most noise about the SIR process, this analysis suggests, are precisely where scrutiny should concentrate first.
