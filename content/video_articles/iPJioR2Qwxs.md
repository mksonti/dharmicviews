Set aside morality and ethics for a moment — this is pure politics, the way it actually runs behind the public rhetoric. Every government gives its citizens one set of reasons for a war and keeps a very different set for itself.

<SectionHeading>What America Actually Is</SectionHeading>

The United States has existed for 250 years and has been at war for roughly 230 to 235 of them — only 15 to 20 years of genuine peace in its entire history, concentrated mostly around the two World Wars. Its resource consumption runs 5 to 6 times what the planet can replenish if the whole world lived at American standards; Bharat, by contrast, consumes less than a single Earth's worth despite its own rapid growth.

<Callout type="info">
The US defense budget is officially $950 billion a year, but total overlay — spread across equipment purchases and research investment — runs closer to $2 trillion, roughly 40% of the entire world's defense spending combined.
</Callout>

<Pullquote>USA has never lost a war — the objectives it tells the public differ from the actual objectives, and by the real metric, every war has succeeded.</Pullquote>

<Divider />

<SectionHeading>Only Three Nations Can Challenge It</SectionHeading>

Measured across GDP, population, and land area together, only three countries have the combination to genuinely challenge the United States.

<ForceCard label="Russia">Vast land mass and historical military parity, but structurally tied down by a war in Ukraine that Western policy helped engineer and sustain.</ForceCard>

<ForceCard label="China">Second-largest economy by nominal GDP, second-most populous, with the industrial base and Belt and Road reach to contest US influence directly.</ForceCard>

<ForceCard label="Bharat">Largest population in the world, a fast-growing GDP, and a civilizational economic history — 30-35% of world GDP for most of recorded history — that the current containment strategy is explicitly designed to prevent from repeating.</ForceCard>

Other large economies — Japan, Indonesia — lack the full combination of land, population, and GDP scale needed to be a genuine peer competitor. The strategic logic, laid out plainly, is that all three challengers need to be neutralized for America to remain the sole superpower — not because they are worse actors, but because that is how the politics runs.

<Divider />

<SectionHeading>How Russia Was Boxed In</SectionHeading>

Russia and Ukraine share over a thousand years of common heritage — effectively brothers, with most of the USSR's leadership historically drawn from Ukraine. NATO's post-1991 promise not to expand eastward was broken almost immediately: four countries added in 1999, six more by 2004, moving steadily to Russia's doorstep. A 2014 coup replaced Ukraine's government, eventually installing Volodymyr Zelensky, and set the current war in motion.

<Callout type="warning">
Europe simultaneously froze roughly $300 billion in Russian assets — with an estimated $70 billion already spent by France — while continuing to import Russian LNG, funding both sides of the same war at once.
</Callout>

<Definition term="Triangular Diplomacy">The 1972 Shanghai Communiqué, where the US aligned with China specifically to weaken the Soviet Union — a strategic marriage of convenience that outlived its original target.</Definition>

<Definition term="Self-Fulfilled Prophecy">A justification repeated long enough — Iran being "weeks from a nuclear weapon" for over a decade, since Obama first said it in 2013-14 — that it eventually becomes the pretext for the action it predicted.</Definition>

<Divider />

<SectionHeading>How Bharat Is Contained</SectionHeading>

The pattern repeats with Bharat, just through different tools. The US was the second country to recognize Pakistan in 1947 — within a day of Iran, the first. Pakistan later received Major Non-NATO Ally status, and Washington threatened to nuclear-strike India during the 1971 Bangladesh war, even as its own diplomat, Archer Blood, sent a cable describing the events as genocide two weeks after Pakistan's crackdown began.

<Pullquote>Trade sanctions, tariffs, technology blockades on jet engines and computing — the key point is Bharat cannot grow, because it is a challenger.</Pullquote>

Add to this a long list of pressure groups — from missionary networks to Khalistani separatists — that find funding and platform whenever they oppose Bharat's rise, regardless of ideology. The throughline is not moral judgment; it's pure strategic containment.

<Divider />

<SectionHeading>The Strait of Hormuz Is the Real Target</SectionHeading>

The heart of the matter sits in oil geography. Roughly 37.7% of all oil leaving the Strait of Hormuz goes to China, and 14.7% goes to Bharat — together accounting for 32% and 42% of each country's respective net oil consumption. LNG flows are similarly lopsided: 33.3% of Hormuz LNG goes to China, 20% to Bharat, representing 27% of China's consumption and a full 50-65% of what Bharat imports.

<Callout type="note">
By contrast, the European Union draws only 13% of its oil and 8% of its LNG through Hormuz, and a mere 1-2% of its goods trade. Striking the strait barely touches Europe — it is engineered almost entirely around China and Bharat.
</Callout>

Layer onto this a set of deepening ties: China's 25-year strategic partnership with Iran (2021) worth an intended $400 billion in Belt and Road investment, Bharat's 10-year contract to develop Iran's Chabahar Port (2024) as an alternative to routes through Pakistan, and Russia's 20-year strategic pact with Iran (2025). Iran has become the shared artery for all three of America's named challengers — which is precisely why it, not Israel or ideology, sits at the center of the current war.

<Divider />

<SectionHeading>Will the War End?</SectionHeading>

Not while China and Bharat continue thriving — and the prediction offered is a full-fledged war by 2032, driven by simple logic: if softer containment tools (tariffs, technology blockades, funded internal dissent) fail, war becomes the last resort, and America has never gone twenty consecutive years without one.

<Pullquote>Bharat holds a pivotal card — whichever side it joins is likely to win — but the pressure to force that choice, rather than allow genuine non-alignment, is exactly what current US policy is applying.</Pullquote>

On the domestic legal mechanics enabling repeated strikes without full congressional approval: the War Powers Resolution of 1973 requires only a 48-hour notification to Congress and permits 60 days of unilateral deployment before requiring approval — a window that resets with each new ceasefire-and-restart cycle, a pattern used by multiple administrations, not unique to any one president.

<Callout type="tip">
The optimism for Bharat isn't built on any single leader. What looks like a single-person project in other systems is, in Bharat's current governing party, an ecosystem with deep bench strength — the strategic direction is expected to continue regardless of who eventually succeeds the current leadership.
</Callout>
